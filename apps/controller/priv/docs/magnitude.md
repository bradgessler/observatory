# Magnitude, in plain words

Astronomers measure brightness backwards: **smaller is brighter**, and negative
numbers are the brightest of all. Each step of 1 is about 2.5× in brightness,
so 5 steps is 100×.

| magnitude | example |
|---|---|
| −27 | the Sun |
| −13 | full Moon |
| −4 | Venus at its best |
| −1.5 | Sirius, the brightest star |
| 0 | Vega |
| 2 | the Big Dipper's stars |
| 4 | faintest stars from a suburb |
| 6 | faintest stars from a truly dark site |
| 9 | limit for 50 mm binoculars |
| 12 | limit for a 4-inch (100 mm) telescope |
| 14 | limit for an 8-inch (200 mm) telescope |

## What the words mean

Instead of a number, Tonight tells you what to expect **with your equipment,
from your sky**:

- **naked eye, obvious** — you can point at it.
- **naked eye** — visible if you know where to look.
- **naked eye, faint smudge · great in the scope** — a hint to the eye, a real
  object in the eyepiece (Andromeda, the Beehive).
- **easy in the scope** — comfortably within what the aperture shows.
- **in the scope** — there, but not bright.
- **faint, needs dark-adapted eyes** — give it a minute; look slightly to the
  side of it.
- **too faint for this scope** — not tonight with this aperture.
- **washed out by the Moon** — a galaxy or nebula that a bright Moon will
  flatten into the sky glow.

## Why total magnitude lies about fuzzy things

A galaxy's magnitude is *all* its light added up. That light is spread over a
patch of sky, so a "magnitude 6" galaxy can be invisible from a driveway while a
magnitude 9 star is easy. Tonight allows for this: galaxies and nebulae need a
few magnitudes of headroom below what the aperture can reach, clusters a little,
stars and planets none. Planetary nebulae are small and dense, so they get
treated more like stars.

## Rough rule for aperture

Limiting magnitude ≈ 7.5 + 5·log₁₀(aperture in cm), minus a point or two for a
bright suburban sky. A 4-inch gives about 12.5 under a dark sky, about 11 in
town. Double the aperture and you gain about 1.5 magnitudes.
